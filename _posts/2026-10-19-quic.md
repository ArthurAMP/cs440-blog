## Key Idea

There are a few fairly independent contributions in this paper, but the main ones are:

- They leverage the fact that there are some fields in QUIC scans which you can derive the keys to find information about hypergiant deployments through a network telescope (in this paper, CAIDA)

- As QUIC flood attacks spoof their source IP address, they often fall in IP addresses within CAIDAs IP block. Thus, they can use the server's replies to find out more about the targeted server without ever having to perform active measurements (but they actively scanned for validation as well) 

Using these two facts, they were able to find out a lot of previously unknown information, like retry rate (the spoofed ip address never acknowledges replies), retry delay, packet size, and even some offnet servers by finding cases the reply comes from outside the target network. It also is able to count load balancers for Meta deployments, but only because Meta's QUIC implementation, mvfst, is open source. 

## Critique

This paper is hard to evaluate because by construction the things it measures are extremely hard to verify. And its most interesting finding, Meta's load balancers growth, isn't feasible in other deployments AND it has some unaccounted for cofounders, like number of times Meta was attacked in a given year. However, those limitations are fundamental instead of something you can circumvent, and this paper gave us interesting data which cannot be obtained any more precisely without a large amount of costly active scanning. Thus I am not sure if there is a specific thing about their approach I would criticize.  

## Connections

The problem, approach, and challenges this paper goes over all remind me of [Understanding Partial Reachability in the Internet Core](/cs440-blog/2026/09/30/islands-and-peninsulas/), as both depend on passive scanning and making inferences about networks based on incomplete and narrow information.
